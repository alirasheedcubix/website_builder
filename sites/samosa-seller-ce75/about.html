Since 1987

Kept in the family since the first pan.

A samosa house is a small promise: the edge holds, the batch is short, and Monday is quiet.

Farida Qureshi set a pan down at the Palace Gate in 1987. She had potato, pea, cumin, and a square of cloth. Office workers bought one on the way in and, if the batch had not gone cold, one on the way out.

Meher learned the seal at twelve. The edge is pressed with the side of the thumb, not pinched like a pie. Too much water and the crust blisters. Too little and it opens in the oil. She still talks about that edge as if it were a person she has agreed to live with.

The mawa came in a cold December. A customer who had eaten the aloo since the first year asked for something sweet to carry home on the train. Farida cooked milk until it held a spoon, added cardamom, and folded it into the same triangle. It has stayed.

In 2014 the lane outside the gate had become a hurry. They took a room fourteen steps down Chandni Gali. The sign is a tile beside the arch, not a board over the street. The kettle is larger. The batches are not.

What did not move: the chutneys are ground in the morning, the oil is groundnut, and Monday is quiet. The keema earned a second kettle the year a regular asked that the clove not find its way into the potato. It was a fair request. We kept it.

Zafran is the saffron that goes into the keema and the warm milk. It is also just the word fired into the tile at number 14.
